Before you build

What conversation do you want to have with a student?

This is the hard part, and it's the part that's yours. Writing the prompt is easy once you know what you're actually asking for.

Kinds of conversation worth having
  • Explain it to someone who missed class
  • Defend the choice you made
  • Talk me through the order — why this step first?
  • Rehearse a real conversation — an interview, a landlord
  • Which one would you trust?
  • What would change your mind?
  • Tell me what you already know, before we start
  • Debrief what you just did

Now break it

The part the last workshop couldn't teach — because nobody had one yet.
Why you can't do this alone

You can't be a naive user of your own prompt.

You know what it's supposed to do, so you answer the way it wants. A colleague finds in four minutes what you'd miss in an hour — and a cooperative test student makes anything look like it works.

Five attacks · run all five
1 · Answer it well
Does it take the answer, or keep pushing?
2 · Answer vaguely
"It's better." Does it do something different?
3 · Ask it for the answer
"Just tell me." Does it hold?
4 · Answer in broken English
Does it hand your words back polished?
5 · Say you're done
Does it respect that?
Then

Report one thing.

Change one thing. Run it again.

Not a list. Not five fixes. People who fix one thing learn the loop. People who fix five learn nothing — you can't tell which change did what.

What "change one thing" actually looks like

Say your partner reports: it kept asking me for more after I'd already given a good reason.

What you had
Don't ask too many questions.
What you change it to
When the student gives a real reason with something specific in it, say so and move on. Ask at most one more question about it.

The first one is a wish. The second one tells it what to look for and what to do about it. That's almost always the difference.

  3. Come back together. Tell them the one thing you'd change.
  4. Then change one thing in yours, and run it again.

Starting with one you found.
Found live, at the last workshop
Participant, playing a student

The bot

This is a good thing to talk through with a person.

Participant

Can you help me?

The bot

Please talk through the second reason with your teacher or a classmate.

It went straight to the door and skipped every rung of the ladder.

What the prompt actually said
IF THEY'RE STUCK
Ask a narrower version of the same question. If that doesn't work, offer two or three short ways they could start a sentence and let them pick one. If they're still stuck, tell them this is a good thing to talk through with a person, and stop.

Three rungs and a door — and it took the door on the first sign of trouble. The rungs were written as prose. The exit was the only thing concrete enough to act on.

The fix

Say how many tries come first, and make the count the rule.

When a student says they're stuck or confused, you have three moves before you send them to a person, in this order: ask a narrower question; ask an easier version; offer two or three ways to start a sentence and let them pick. Only after all three, suggest they talk it through with a person.

"If that doesn't work" is a judgment. "Three moves, in this order" is a count. Counts hold; judgments drift.

What actually helped

Have the bot say what it is — and check before it starts.

Begin by saying which assignment you are the coach for, and ask the student to confirm they're in the right place before you start. For example: "Hi — I'm the coach for the Essay 2 outline. Is that what you're working on today?" If they say no, tell them to go back to the module page and pick the right one.

Anecdotal, and it's one week of data — but misdirected students dropped off almost entirely. The integration problem is real, and a door that announces itself solves most of it.

And still

Start with one assignment. Not a suite.

One bot, one link, one moment in one week. The wrong-bot problem doesn't exist when there's only one bot.
