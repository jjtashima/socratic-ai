Socratic AI Tutoring · Worksheet

🔀 Build one

Two parts. The first takes five minutes and gives you a working conversation. The second is what makes it teach instead of chat.

This sheet is written for the hardest reader: you, alone, two weeks from now, with nobody to ask. Everything you need is on this page. You don't need to have been in the workshop and you don't need any experience with AI.

Rough is fine. Rough is the point.

What you're making

A Socratic AI tutor is a chatbot following instructions you wrote. The instructions tell it to ask instead of answer, and to keep going until the student has produced something.

You write those instructions in plain English. There's no code. When you're done here, you press a button and this page assembles your answers into instructions you can paste into a chatbot.

Where to try it

chatgpt.com or gemini.google.com — neither needs an account. Fine for testing and for making up practice conversations.

For real student work, use the tool your district has approved. Anything a student actually writes goes there and nowhere else.

Part one

Your first conversation

Four questions. About five minutes. You'll have something that runs.

1

What's this conversation about?

One or two sentences. Where in your course it happens, and what the student is being asked to do.

2

What should the student have by the end?

Something you could point at. This is what tells the conversation when to stop — without it, the bot just keeps being pleasant.

3

What should the student work out on their own?

Name the part that's theirs. The bot can explain anything you've already taught — but whatever goes here, it asks about and never supplies.

4

What's the first question it asks?

How the conversation opens. Something they can answer from where they're standing.

You can stop here, and that's a real outcome. Paste that into a chatbot, be a student for five minutes, and you'll have run a Socratic tutor you wrote.

Part two is what turns it from a conversation into teaching. It takes longer and it's the part only you can do — but it isn't a requirement, and it will still be here next week.

Part two

The Branch

You already know the wrong answers. That's the branch.

Part one gets the bot asking. But it treats every student the same — same follow-up, whoever they are and whatever they said.

Teaching is what happens next: you notice what kind of answer you just got, and you say something different depending on which kind it was. That's the part that looks hard and isn't, because it's built out of something you already carry around.

You can predict the unsatisfying answers. You've been watching them for years.

5

What answer would you be thrilled to get?

Write it the way a real student would write it, not the way you would. Thrilled, not perfect.

6

What answers do you actually get?

The three most common unsatisfying responses. Not the worst ones — the usual ones. Include the ones that are almost right.

7

What would you ask next — for each one?

One question per answer, and a different one each time. This is the whole exercise. If all three get the same question, you don't have a branch yet.

Three things that make a next question work

Don't put the answer inside the question. "Isn't the eyewitness more reliable because they were there?" hands over the reasoning and lets the student agree without thinking. Students take any wording you offer — usually word for word.

Make it answerable from where they are. A question that needs something they don't have yet produces silence, not thought.

Different answers get different questions. Same question three times is a chatbot. Three different questions is teaching.

If you're stuck on question 7
  1. Look back at what's missing. You wrote it in question 6, and the next question is usually hiding in it. "No reason behind it" → what made it the right one for you? "Won't commit" → if you had to pick one, which?
  2. Write a rough one anyway. Even a bad question tells you what the good one needs to do.
  3. Still stuck? Ask a chatbot — but ask for choices. Paste in your three usual answers and say: for each one, give me three different questions I could ask next. Don't put the answer inside the question, and make the three sets different from each other. Then pick. Asking for one answer means taking it; asking for three means choosing, and the choosing is yours.
  4. Then check. Are your three questions actually different from each other? That's the thing an AI is most likely to get wrong.

Use the typical answers you wrote down — not a real student's actual words.

When you try it: five things to watch for

Talk to your own bot for ten minutes, and be a difficult student on purpose — a cooperative test student will make anything look like it works. Then watch for these. All five are ordinary and all five are fixable.

  • It kept pushing after a good answer. The most common one by a wide margin. A student who already said something real gets asked for more, and reads that as "I keep getting this wrong." Tell it plainly: when the answer is good enough to use, say so and move on.
  • It wrote the student's sentence for them. Watch the summarizing moments especially — "so what you're saying is…" is where a bot quietly upgrades rough words into polished ones and hands them back as theirs. In real transcripts this happens most to the students whose English is least standard, which means the writers who most need their own words are the ones most likely to lose them.
  • It asked and then answered itself in the same breath. "Do you want to add that? Great, here's the list." That isn't a question, it's an announcement. If a step is supposed to wait for the student, it has to be its own turn.
  • It said "I hear you, but" and did the thing anyway. That phrase is a reliable signal that two of your instructions collided and you never said which one wins. Don't make either one louder — a tie can't be broken by shouting. Write down which one governs. A good default: if the student says they're done with something, that ends it.
  • It judged the student instead of the answer. Never ask a bot to decide whether a student is ready, able, or strong enough. It can only see the words on the screen, and fluent-sounding writing reads as good thinking whether or not it is. Judge the turn in front of it, not the person behind it.

Six filled-in examples

Different courses, same questions. Open the closest one to your teaching and change it into yours. Starting from an example isn't cheating — it's faster, and it works.

Beginning ESL — choosing between two apartments
What it's about
Students see two apartment listings and say which one they'd pick and why. Short — six turns, and speaking the answer is fine.
By the end they have
A choice and one reason, in their own words.
Theirs to figure out
Which apartment fits their own life, and why.
Opening question
"Which apartment would you choose?"
What I'd be thrilled to get
"I pick the second one. It is more money but it is close to the bus. I don't have a car so the bus is important for my job."
What I actually get
"The second one is better." No reason at all.
"The second one because it is nice." A reason word, but nothing specific.
"The second one has two bedrooms and a parking space." Copies the listing instead of choosing.
What I'd ask next
1 → "What makes it better for you?"
2 → "What is one thing about it that is nice?"
3 → "That is what the ad says. Which of those things do you need?"
High school / HSE social studies — which account to trust
What it's about
After reading two short accounts of the same event, students say which they trust more and why.
By the end they have
A choice and two reasons for it.
Theirs to figure out
What makes a source more or less trustworthy here.
Opening question
"Which of the two accounts did you find more believable?"
What I'd be thrilled to get
"The second one, because the writer was there and they say who else was in the room. The first one doesn't say where they got it from."
What I actually get
"The second one, it's more reliable." The judgment with no reasoning under it.
"They both have good points." Won't commit, so there's nothing to defend.
"The second one says the first one got the date wrong." Repeats the source instead of judging it.
What I'd ask next
1 → "What is it about that one that makes it reliable to you?"
2 → "If you had to put one in a report, which one goes in?"
3 → "That's what it claims. What makes you think it's right about that?"
Career training — why this order of steps
What it's about
Students can perform the procedure correctly but can't say why the steps go in that order — so they can't adapt when something is different.
By the end they have
A reason for the order that isn't "that's the checklist."
Theirs to figure out
What actually goes wrong if the order changes.
Opening question
"Why does that step come first?"
What I'd be thrilled to get
"You check it first because if you do it after, you've already contaminated the thing you were checking."
What I actually get
"Because that's the order on the checklist." Authority, not reasoning.
"Because it's safer." True and untestable — could be said about any step.
"I don't know, that's just how we learned it." Honest, and stuck.
What I'd ask next
1 → "What would go wrong if you did it the other way around?"
2 → "Safer for who, and from what?"
3 → "Try it out loud — what do you think would happen if you skipped it?"
Active adults — a decision worth talking through
What it's about
Students talk through a real decision they've made and what they'd tell someone facing the same one. Nothing is graded.
By the end they have
One piece of advice they'd actually give.
Theirs to figure out
What the experience taught them.
Opening question
"What's a decision you made that you still think about?"
What I'd be thrilled to get
"I'd tell them to wait a season before deciding. I moved too fast and I spent a year fixing it."
What I actually get
"It worked out fine." Closes the topic instead of opening it.
"Everybody's different, you have to do what's right for you." True, and it says nothing.
A long story with no takeaway. Rich material, no conclusion drawn.
What I'd ask next
1 → "Was there a moment where it could have gone another way?"
2 → "What was right for you, then?"
3 → "If a friend were about to do the same thing, what's the one thing you'd say?"
Social science (credit) — defending an interpretation
What it's about
Students state an interpretation of a case and hold it against the strongest counterexample.
By the end they have
A revised claim that survives the counterexample, or a reason it doesn't apply.
Theirs to figure out
Whether the counterexample sinks the claim or narrows it.
Opening question
"Here's the case that cuts against you. What does it do to your claim?"
What I'd be thrilled to get
"That case does cut against me. But it's a different scale, and my claim was about local decisions — so it narrows what I'm saying rather than sinking it."
What I actually get
"That's a good point, I hadn't thought of that." Concedes instantly instead of testing.
Restates the original claim more forcefully. Doesn't engage the counterexample.
"Well, it depends how you define the terms." Retreats into definitions to avoid the case.
What I'd ask next
1 → "Does it sink your claim, or narrow it? What's left standing?"
2 → "Where does that case fit in what you just said?"
3 → "Pick the definition you meant. Does the case still bite?"
Health & kinesiology (credit) — justifying a recommendation
What it's about
Given a specific case, students recommend an approach and say what would change their mind.
By the end they have
A recommendation, a reason tied to this case, and a condition that would change it.
Theirs to figure out
Which details of the case are doing the work.
Opening question
"What would you start with for this person?"
What I'd be thrilled to get
"I'd start conservative because of the history. If the pain didn't drop in two weeks I'd rethink it — that would tell me I read the cause wrong."
What I actually get
The recommendation with no reasoning. Right answer, invisible thinking.
A textbook rule quoted back. General principle, not applied to this case.
"Nothing would change my mind." Not a position — an unfalsifiable one.
What I'd ask next
1 → "What is it about this case that pointed you there?"
2 → "What in this person's history makes that rule apply?"
3 → "What would you have to see for this to be the wrong call?"
