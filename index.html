The three things

If you remember nothing else from the session:

  1. Say what the conversation should produce. It ends when a thing exists — not when the student runs out of steam.
  2. Tell it what to do with each kind of answer. A prompt that reacts is a chatbot. A prompt that sorts is a teacher.
  3. Tell it to take a good answer when it gets one. The most common failure isn't being too easy. It's being too relentless.

Two things worth knowing before you use it with students

Somebody will eventually tell it something serious. If the hard thing is the subject of the work — something they lived through and are writing about — one warm sentence, then keep coaching. If it's about right now, stop coaching, stay warm, don't probe, and name a person. And put the real offices and phone numbers in the prompt itself; left to improvise, it will invent plausible wrong ones.

Real student writing goes only through the approved tool. When you're testing your own prompt, make up the student answers yourself. Taking the name off isn't enough — a paragraph about someone's family, or the field they're going into, identifies them without it.

There's no next level you have to reach. If all you ever do is write down three wrong answers and one question for each, and ask those questions yourself in class, you've got the whole idea. The chatbot is a way to do it thirty times at once. It isn't the point.
